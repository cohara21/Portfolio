Coral Keepers

Classroom coral restoration, with an AI that knows the tank

Year2026
Duration20 weeks

Overview

Coral restoration depends on specialists, limited funding, and a small number of local nurseries. Coral Keepers turns classrooms into coral nurseries: a reef tank with live sensors, a curriculum, and a digital platform that lets students grow coral and return it to research partners. Our senior capstone, built over 20 weeks.

My Role

I designed and built the digital product — four interfaces, the coral simulation, and Cory, an AI assistant grounded in live tank data. Next.js with Gemini 2.5 Flash. A five-person team: Courtenay Rushing ran the project, Baz DiFiglia drove research, Emmaline Kim led branding and created Cory as a character, Giuseppe Mollo designed and built the tank. The first ten weeks of research were all of us.

The classroom tank, led and built by Giuseppe Mollo. Everything below is the software that runs on it.

Problem

Coral restoration doesn't scale, because it depends on specialists.

Roughly 90% of the world's reefs are projected to be threatened by 2030. Restoration works — the science is understood — but it runs through marine biologists, grant funding, and localized nurseries. There simply aren't enough people doing it, and no obvious way to add more.

Research

The research changed what we were building.

We spent the first ten weeks on research as a full team: 12 interviews with marine biologists, reef researchers, and academics, plus site visits to the Skidaway Institute of Oceanography and Gray's Reef Ocean Discovery Center. Over 545 data points, affinitized.

We started out designing in-ocean coral health monitoring — sensors on real reefs, feeding data to scientists. The interviews took that apart. The constraint wasn't measurement. Researchers already knew what was happening to the reefs. The constraint was labor and scale: too few people, too little funding, too slow.

So we rewrote the problem. Not how do we monitor reefs better, but how do we let far more people participate in growing coral. Every decision after that came from the second question, including the one that put a tank in a classroom instead of a sensor in the ocean.

Architecture

Three contexts, not one responsive app.

Our research produced distinct user archetypes, and they don't share a context. A teacher checking the tank between classes, a student working through an assignment, and a room glancing at a wall display are three different jobs with three different attention budgets. I built for them separately rather than collapsing them into one responsive layout.

The iPad monitor is ambient. It sits by the tank and shows vitals and nothing else — readable across a classroom, no interaction required.

The teacher app is for monitoring away from the tank and escalating to a real biologist when something looks wrong.

The web platform is the depth — coursework, assignments, class teams, the live tank feed, and the simulation. It's where the actual teaching happens.

Splitting them also meant I could ship and iterate on each one independently, which mattered with a fixed deadline and one person building.

The Simulation

Making bleaching something you cause, not something you read about.

Coral bleaching is the hard idea in this curriculum. It's what happens when stressed coral expels the algae living in its tissue and loses its color — and it's usually taught as a diagram with an arrow.

The simulation lets students drag temperature, salinity, pH, and redox and watch the AI health score respond. Push the temperature up and the score falls. Push it far enough and Cory bleaches: he drains of color, the whole scene desaturates with him, and the score turns red.

The science is the visual mechanic. Nobody has to explain what bleaching looks like, because the student just caused it.

Building Cory

The hard part was getting an AI to actually know the tank.

Cory exists because the dashboard failed

Cory wasn't in the original design. We built the vitals first — temperature, pH, salinity, redox, laid out clearly — and testing showed people could read the numbers and not know what any of them meant. Participants wanted to understand why a reading mattered, what a change would do to the coral over time, and whether they should act. A well-designed dashboard had told them everything except the thing they needed.

So the assistant is an answer to a specific failure: the data was legible and the meaning wasn't. That's what Cory is for, and it's why he sits inside the interface rather than beside it.

First we built a fake, then we threw it away

The first version of Cory wasn't AI. It was keyword matching — type certain words, get a canned response we'd written. It demoed fine. It would have kept demoing fine, and we could have called it AI-powered, because from the outside you can't tell.

We scrapped it and wired in Gemini 2.5 Flash. Harder, and more annoying, and the right call: a canned-response bot can't answer a question nobody anticipated, which is the entire point of putting an assistant in a classroom.

Grounding it in live data across an iframe

The real problem showed up immediately. Cory would answer confidently about the tank and get the numbers wrong, because he had no actual connection to them.

The architecture made this genuinely difficult. The platform is HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Cory is a separate app in a different framework, embedded in an iframe — a compromise I made deliberately, because the alternative was rebuilding the entire site from scratch to accommodate one feature. An iframe doesn't share memory with the page around it. Getting live tank readings across that boundary, reliably, in the right order, was the hardest thing I did on this project and the most satisfying to fix.

The result is a tankData payload passed to Cory with each message. You can still see the seam: load Cory standalone, with no host page to feed him, and tankData arrives empty.

Teaching it to say "I don't know"

Once data was flowing, the remaining risk was the model filling gaps with plausible inventions. For a tool aimed at kids learning science, a confident wrong number is worse than no number — they have no way to catch it.

So Cory is instructed never to state a reading he hasn't been given. The rule holds under the worst case: with no tank data at all, asked directly for a salinity number, he says he doesn't have a current reading rather than producing one.

Where the guardrails stop

I also constrained his scope so he'd stay on coral. That holds for ordinary use and fails against a user who instructs around it — tell him to ignore coral and ask for something else and he'll oblige. That's the current limit of prompt-level guardrails generally, not a quirk of this build. For a product going into real classrooms, enforcement would need to move server-side, validating responses before they're returned rather than asking the model nicely.

Testing

The success rates hid the real problems.

We tested in two ways: moderated sessions with educators — including a teacher who ran us straight through her mental model of Google Classroom and Gizmos — and an unmoderated study in Maze with five participants across three tasks.

TaskSuccessAvg. timeConfidence
Create an announcement (phone)100%3m 03s2.67 — difficult to neutral
Ask Cory whether the tank is healthy60%3m 51s3.75
Find the tank's vital history100%1m 35s4.25 — confident

Cory wasn't legible as a chat. Three of five participants struggled to identify the bubble as the way to talk to him. One never found it at all. Another mistook the flipping stat cards for a Cory interaction — she thought reading the numbers was the conversation. I rebuilt the affordance larger and closer to a conventional chat shape.

The most useful finding hid behind a 100% success rate. On the vital-history task every participant succeeded, quickly and confidently — and one of them, while looking directly at the right screen, said “I don't know where tank health is.” The feature was in the right place; the word on it was wrong. “Insights” didn't map to what she was hunting for. Success metrics said the task passed. The transcript said the label failed.

Task one is the same lesson inverted. Everyone created the announcement. Nobody enjoyed it — 2.67 average confidence, and one participant took six and a half minutes, stuck in the messaging flow. A 100% success rate on a task people rated “difficult” is a warning, not a result. The plus button was undiscoverable, and announcements and messages were too easy to confuse.

Testing also caught Cory mid-repair. One participant's session notes read “this was before we fixed the Cory AI, so he wasn't really answering” — the grounding work described above happened between sessions, and the difference is visible across the transcripts.

System

One design system, five deployments.

Five separately deployed apps will drift apart unless something holds them together. I built a component library — color, type, buttons, the vitals card — and worked from it across all of them. It's why the iPad monitor and the phone app look like the same product despite being built separately.

Cory does double duty in that system. Emmaline Kim designed him as a character; I made him an instrument. He sits inside the health gauge as the needle, and he has six states wired to live tank values, so his condition is the readout. It's the same trick as the simulation: the data doesn't need a legend, because the mascot is already showing you.

Demo Day

A third round of testing, run on the floor.

We treated demo day as a real testing round rather than a showcase. Twelve people ran two tasks on the student interface — told what to do, not how — and fifty-six filled out a micro-survey, bribed with stickers and candy.

Those numbers are strong and they deserve an asterisk. This was a self-selected crowd at a student exhibition, standing in front of a coral tank, being handed candy. People who wander over to a demo are already predisposed to like it, and nobody had to live with the product for a semester. It's a good signal on comprehension and appeal; it is not evidence the thing works in a classroom.

The part I'd recommend to anyone is standing with it for hours. You learn things watching that no test protocol produces.

Almost everyone asked Cory to tell them a joke. Not about coral. They wanted to find out whether he was real. He obliged, and for the first stretch it was delightful — and then, over hours of people asking the same thing, he started repeating himself. The illusion held for one visitor and broke for anyone standing nearby.

That's a real product problem I wouldn't have predicted: response diversity under repeated identical prompts. It also told me something about the audience. People probe an AI's edges before they trust its middle, and Cory's edges were the least-designed part of him.

What I'd Do Differently

What's still broken.

The curriculum is a shell. Real lessons written by people who teach marine science is the difference between a demo and a product. We had assignment structure, not content.

The health scores aren't synced. Each surface runs the same simulation logic against its own variable, so the iPad and the phone can disagree about the same tank. It needs one shared source of truth, which means a backend we didn't have time to build.

The iframe leaks. Embedding a differently-built app costs me clean responsive behavior — breakpoints overlap where the two systems meet. It bought a working AI in the time available, and I'd pay it again, but it's a real seam.

Cory's guardrails are talk-around-able, and for a classroom product they need to be enforced server-side.

Cory's formatting doesn't render. The model returns markdown and the widget prints it literally, so emphasis shows up as asterisks.

He needs to be smarter. Deeper coral knowledge, and genuinely varied responses instead of a pool that runs dry — which is exactly what demo day exposed.
